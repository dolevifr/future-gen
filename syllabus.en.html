28 sessions

Semester 1

A creative AI studio

13 sessions

Part 1Visual AI — from a first prompt to a brand

1Words that cast spells — an intro to AI and prompts
What they learn

What is a prompt? A deep dive into the world of AI and the "magic formula": role, task, rules and style. Kids learn to talk to machines and turn their imagination into precise instructions that create stunning images in seconds — just like ordering a dish from a robot chef.

What they do

"Super-prompt quest": kids practise writing prompts against a giant stopwatch to create their own superhero in Bing Image Creator.

2"Going to print" — a consistent look for a comic book
What they learn

Keeping a consistent visual style and joining separate images into one storyline with Canva. Kids learn why a unified visual language is what makes a story or a brand look professional, and why dropping a 3D character into a pixel world is a recipe for brand failure.

What they do

Creating a short comic strip that keeps the same visual language.

3The branding studio — the poster and the brand name
What they learn

The psychology of branding, colour theory and how colours affect the subconscious. How luxury brands behave compared with everyday brands, how to put text inside AI images, how to design a clean, catchy logo, and how white space signals real professionalism.

What they do

"My brand": creating a brand with AI tools — a virtual shop, colours that express what the founder means, a product design and a logo.

Part 2Making the product and the perfect presentation

4The school hack — secrets of the perfect presentation
What they learn

Using AI (such as NotebookLM and Canva) as a personal research assistant for stunning presentations. Kids learn the design rules of a winning presentation (the 5x5 rule, contrast), how to summarise information fast without boring copy-paste, and how to turn any school project into a presentation that keeps the audience awake.

What they do

"The hero file": kids build a four-slide presentation about their hero or brand, using the images and logo they made in earlier sessions.

5Making a video and animating the product
What they learn

Into the director's chair: producing an advert from scratch with AI. Kids learn to identify a problem and build a precise storyboard for it with Gemini, write a script, work with the ten-seconds-per-scene rule in Google Vids, and add professional narration to launch their product.

What they do

Producing the first promo video for their product or hero.

Part 3Action! Turning pictures into films

6Bringing pictures to life — AI animation
What they learn

The world of generative video. Kids see how advanced AI tools analyse a flat still image, understand its depth and add movement and life to it, creating short, breathtaking action clips made for social media.

What they do

"Move the hero": generating 3–4 second action clips from their best images.

7Video editing — putting the puzzle together
What they learn

The basics of professional video editing in smart apps (such as CapCut). Kids learn the power of the cut in cinema — how jumping between camera angles creates tension, pace and interest — and how to build a clear storyline with transitions.

What they do

"The editing room": joining the short clips into one logical sequence, ordering the story and adding transitions.

8Putting it all together into a full video
What they learn

The last stage of production: images, video, transcription, captions and sound come together. Kids see how the soundtrack sets the viewer's emotions, and how dramatic music versus upbeat music can completely change the meaning of the very same scene.

What they do

"The official trailer": bringing the song into CapCut, adding narration with text-to-speech and producing an epic 30-second trailer.

Part 4The final project — on the way to the Oscars

9The secret mission — the hackathon begins
What they learn

Kicking off the final project in teams of three, and practising teamwork and brainstorming under time pressure.

What they do

Teams get a wild theme for a new story (say, "the pizza invasion from outer space"), write a shared plot and generate the base images.

10Creative sprint 1 — visuals and sound
What they learn

Managing a project and splitting the roles in a team, just like a tech company. Each team turns its idea into a complete visual brand with its own soundtrack.

What they do

Teams work on their own to create the film's characters, worlds and soundtrack.

11Creative sprint 2 — video and editing
What they learn

Turning 2D into cinema: intensive hands-on work in the editing room, with strict deadlines to finish the film.

What they do

Animating the images from the previous session, exporting the sound and editing the final film in CapCut.

12The preview screening and polish
What they learn

Peer review and quality assurance (QA). Kids learn to give and take constructive criticism — what works and what's missing — and see that a good product goes through several rounds of fixes before it reaches the public.

What they do

Screening the films to the class, getting feedback from friends and making the last fixes in the editing room.

13The FutureGen OscarsShowcase event

The showcase that closes semester one: presenting to an audience and celebrating what they achieved. The kids show their parents how they made their work, using the presentations they built in session 4, and proudly screen the AI films and comics they created.

Semester 2

From a gaming studio to a first startup

15 sessions

Part 1What makes a game fun? Design and logic

14The anatomy of a good game (game design)
What they learn

The psychology of video games: challenge, reward and losing. Kids analyse famous games and see how great games teach the rules without a single word — through level design alone.

What they do

"The worst game in the world": playing a deliberately broken game to find what's wrong with it and suggest fixes to its rules.

15Designing the game assets
What they learn

Creating sprites — the 2D graphics every game is made of. The secret behind the scenes: every video game is, in the end, a collection of drawings with transparent backgrounds that move on command.

What they do

Kids design their hero, enemy and coin with AI, remove the backgrounds and get them ready for the game.

16First steps in the game engine
What they learn

Meeting a no-code development environment and the most basic programming logic: if/then. Kids discover that without physics rules set in advance, like gravity or solid ground, the character simply falls into a virtual abyss.

What they do

Opening a new project, importing the characters and setting up the move and jump buttons.

Part 2Building the world — levels and enemies

17Designing the first level (level design)
What they learn

Virtual architecture and user-experience design: an obstacle course that starts easy, to give the player a sense of success, and grows challenging but fair.

What they do

Building the floors, walls and pits. Each kid builds a course that needs precise jumps and planning.

18Here come the bad guys — enemy AI
What they learn

Programming automatic behaviour: commands that move several objects on their own at the same time, to make the game world feel alive.

What they do

Adding the enemy to the game and giving it an automatic patrol route, left and right.

Part 3Building and marketing a product — a founder's mindset

19Entrepreneurial thinking and spotting opportunities
What they learn

Developing a founder's mindset: how founders think, how they spot business opportunities where others see only problems and frustration, and what the case studies of history's great founders teach.

What they do

A team brainstorm to find burning everyday problems that could have a technological or physical solution.

20Product discovery and identifying problems
What they learn

Discovery in modern product management: how to define a problem in depth, understand who suffers from it, why market research comes before writing a single line of code, and why the most successful tech companies fall in love with the problem before the solution.

What they do

Building a user profile and researching a real problem the kids chose to solve in their final project.

21The world of MVP and POC
What they learn

The key ideas of product development: what a minimum viable product (MVP) is and what a proof of concept (POC) is. Agile principles, why smart founders don't wait for a perfect product before launching, and how to test whether an idea is worth it with minimum time and money.

What they do

Defining and sketching the MVP of the new product the teams came up with.

Part 4"Shark Tank" — founding the venture and the big raise

22The art of the investor deck (pitch deck)
What they learn

From an idea to a multi-million business: the structure of a Silicon Valley–style pitch deck — the problem, the solution, the market size and the competitive edge — and how to write a one-pager that sums up the startup on a single page.

What they do

Building the backbone of the team's investor deck.

23The pitch — facing an audience and speaking money
What they learn

Sharpening leadership, charisma and rhetoric: techniques for getting a message across and persuading, reading body language, answering tough investor questions under pressure, and using financial terms (investment, shares, return on investment) to show the sharks you speak business.

What they do

A first run of the elevator pitch in front of the group, with feedback for improvement.

24Development hackathon, stage 1 — identity and branding
What they learn

The final work sprint begins with a corporate identity for the kids' company. Each team manages its own time, splits the roles (CEO, product manager, head of design) and uses AI tools for all of its business branding.

What they do

Choosing a name and creating a logo, a colour palette and a winning slogan for the startup.

25Development hackathon, stage 2 — proof of concept and marketing
What they learn

Creating marketing assets that prove to investors the product is amazing (POC). The kids use the whole toolbox they have built up: AI promo videos, product animations, mock-ups and market data gathered with ChatGPT.

What they do

Producing the main marketing materials around the product.

26Development hackathon, stage 3 — closing the pitch deck
What they learn

Heading for the finish line: teams take their visuals, financial figures and market research and pack it all into the investor deck and the one-pager.

What they do

Final design and polish of the decks with graphic tools, sticking to the 5x5 rule.

27Dress rehearsal — the CEOs on stage
What they learn

Mental and technical preparation for the fundraising event: handling pressure, controlling the tone of voice, using gestures for winning body language, and sharing the speaking time between the partners.

What they do

A full on-stage run of the presentation in front of the mentor, for the final polish before the big moment.

28"Shark Tank" — demo dayShowcase event

The year's finale, where it all comes together: entrepreneurship, AI, leadership and finance meet the real world. The classroom becomes an investment arena, and each team of young founders presents its startup. With the pitch deck they built and a captivating performance, they try to convince a panel of investors — mentors and parents — to invest in their idea.

Semester 1

Branding, content and AI studio

12 sessions

Part 1The branding studio and visual AI

1Words that cast spells — commercial prompt engineering
What they learn

The role, task, rules and style formula. A live demo shows the huge gap between a simple prompt ("cool sneakers") and a professional one with a lens, studio lighting and commercial product photography.

What they do

"The launch collection": each student or team invents a fashion or gadget brand and produces a collection of three photorealistic product shots at commercial quality.

2"The product speaks" — a digital AI presenter
What they learn

How a still image of a character comes to life: type a short marketing script, and at the click of a button the character blinks and speaks in a completely realistic voice.

What they do

"The digital presenter": students write a short (ten-second) sales script in ChatGPT, create an influencer or ambassador character with AI, and produce a short clip where the character speaks and recommends the product.

3The branding studio — logo and launch poster
What they learn

How to get Ideogram to produce a clean vector logo with accurate text, bring it into Canva and pair it with clean Hebrew fonts (such as Rubik, Heebo and Assistant) in an advertising poster — with examples of how businesses use posters.

What they do

"The launch poster": creating an official logo and placing it in a full-size campaign poster for the product they developed.

Part 2The production studio and the pitch

4Playing the brand — an AI mini-game
What they learn

What gamification is, and why brands like McDonald's and Red Bull build games to win an audience. In a live demo, AI builds a simple browser game — catch the falling products, a quick brand quiz or a branded Flappy Bird — that runs within two minutes.

What they do

"The brand's game": students feed the AI their brand's world, generate an interactive mini-game, change its colours and rules with prompts, and get a link they can play on a phone or a computer.

5Pitch secrets — an investor deck in seconds
What they learn

Type one paragraph about a venture idea and get a designed five-slide deck at the click of a button — then learn to edit and sharpen the content by hand.

What they do

"The 4-slide pitch": each team feeds in its brand details, generates a deck, adds the images and logo it made, and builds a short investor deck: problem, solution, market and product.

Part 3Action! Turning a brand into viral video

6Bringing pictures to life — AI video animation
What they learn

A still product image from the first session, a camera move (pan or zoom) or a motion prompt — and within two minutes the image becomes a smoothly moving 3D video.

What they do

"Virtual studio shoots": students animate three key brand images and export short shots (B-roll) for an advert.

7Short-form video editing (Reels / TikTok)
What they learn

A video editor's workspace: importing clips, fast jump cuts, dynamic transitions and building a hook that grabs the viewer in the first three seconds.

What they do

"The editing room": joining the animated clips into a fast, punchy 20–30 second clip.

Part 4Final project — a branding and launch hackathon

8Launching the hackathon — the business brief and campaign plan

Teams of three get a practical brief: launching a new brand for teenagers. Each team brainstorms, splits the roles and opens a shared project file.

9Production sprint 1 — design, logo and visual language

A sprint with a clear timetable: teams produce all of the brand's graphic assets — logo, banners and launch poster.

10Production sprint 2 — video advert and jingle

Last tips on editing sound and video, then to work: teams put the video together, record narration and export the final film.

11Pre-launch — peer review (QA) and polish

Students learn how to give professional, constructive feedback. Teams show each other their drafts, collect comments and lock a final version.

12Finale — FutureGen Creators SummitShowcase event

A celebration evening for parents and guests that closes semester one. Students screen their adverts and present the brands they built and their business decks.

Semester 2

Start-up & Money Lab — entrepreneurship, product and finance

16 sessions

Part 1Entrepreneurial thinking, product management and market research

13Hunting the competition — competitive intelligence and market research with AI
What they learn

How to check whether your idea already exists, and what an unfair advantage is. In a live demo we investigate an existing competitor: pulling its weaknesses out of bad App Store reviews, analysing market prices and finding the gap the competitors missed.

What they do

"The business spy file": each team finds three direct and indirect competitors in its field and produces a comparison table of features and prices that shows how its venture is different and better.

14Product management 1 — discovery studio: turning a problem into an opportunity
What they learn

The iron law of product: "don't fall in love with the solution — fall in love with the problem". How product managers find out what bothers people: analysing complaints and reviews, user interviews, and the difference between a burning pain and a minor inconvenience. The tools: an empathy map, and turning an everyday teen problem into a precise problem statement.

What they do

"Pain hunters": each team picks a field (gaming, school, money for teens, social life), researches pain points online with AI, fills a FigJam board with five key pain points and defines the main user (persona) it is building for.

15Product management 2 — from brainstorm to MVP
What they learn

How to sift 100 ideas down to the core: brainstorming with "How might we…?", what an MVP (minimum viable product) is through the car-versus-skateboard example, and a prioritisation matrix (value to the user versus development effort) that separates must-haves from nice-to-haves.

What they do

"The MVP sprint": teams generate ten possible solutions to the pain they defined, rank them in the matrix and define in one line the MVP they will build — the one feature that must work in version one.

16Product management 3 — the PRD: from idea to spec
What they learn

How developers and designers know what to build: the product requirements document (PRD) — background and goal, user stories ("as a user I want X so that I can Y"), functional requirements and success criteria — and turning an abstract idea into a backlog of tasks with a structured prompt.

What they do

"Writing the instruction manual": each team writes a one-page PRD for its venture — three critical user stories, how the MVP will work, and a task list for development.

17Product management 4 — Agile and running sprints
What they learn

How teams at Google, Spotify and Meta move fast without getting lost: Agile principles — short cycles, a Kanban board (To Do, In Progress, Review, Done), a daily stand-up and a retrospective.

What they do

"The first sprint goes live": teams open a Trello board, enter the tasks from the PRD, assign roles and owners (design, copy, website) and simulate a first development sprint.

Part 2Building the product and the marketing — no-code and funnels

18A digital shop window — a no-code landing page
What they learn

What a landing page is and how it draws customers: a catchy headline, the product, the images from semester one and a call-to-action button. A live demo of editing, fitting it to phones and publishing the site.

What they do

"The site is live": teams design a landing page for their venture, add their visual language and images, check it on a phone and publish a working link.

19What's a backend? How a website thinks and stores data
What they learn

Frontend (what you see on screen, like a restaurant menu) versus backend (the kitchen and the storeroom). What happens the moment you press "send": how the data travels, gets stored in a database, and how sites like Instagram and TikTok remember who we are.

What they do

"Connecting the brain to the site": students embed a form in their landing page, connect it to a data table, send details from their phones and watch the data flow from frontend to backend in real time.

20Monetisation secrets — how do free games make billions?
What they learn

Fortnite and Brawl Stars are completely free — so how does Epic Games make billions? Taking business models apart through gaming: the creator economy and marketplace fees (Roblox), subscriptions (Spotify, Xbox Game Pass) and rewarded ads in mobile games.

What they do

"Cracking the venture's profit engine": teams define their monetisation model in three layers — what users get for free, what exactly they pay for, and a simple price table — and present it in a "battle of the models" in front of the class.

Part 3Real-world finance — salary, tax and the economy

21"The Israeli financial code" — salary, tax and business
What they learn

A concentrated lab: gross versus net, the minimum wage for young workers and travel allowance; income tax brackets and tax credit points — including busting the myth that moving up a bracket leaves you with less; why VAT is an indirect tax a business only collects and passes on, and what national insurance gives you; and exempt versus licensed businesses, and what a deductible expense is.

What they do

"The CEO and employee challenge": in the first half students read a payslip and work out the net pay; in the second they price a business product (cost plus VAT) and decide which deductible expenses offset tax.

22How does money make money? The stock market and shares
What they learn

What a share is, and how buying a fraction of one makes us owners of companies like Apple, Tesla or Nvidia. Capital gains versus dividends, what the S&P 500 is and why betting on a single share is riskier than a broad basket, and investor psychology: why markets rise and fall, and the difference between long-term investing and speculation.

What they do

"The $100,000 portfolio challenge": each team gets a virtual budget, researches 3–4 companies from different fields and builds a diversified portfolio in a spreadsheet, explaining every choice.

23Monopoly in the real world — property investment
What they learn

Why property is the most popular investment: monthly rent (cash flow) versus value growth over the years, the simple yield formula, the power and risks of leverage (a mortgage), and types of property — homes, commercial, holiday lets and REITs. A live demo checks real transaction prices.

What they do

"Investment flat hunters": teams pick a city in Israel, find a potential investment flat and work out the annual yield and the equity needed in a spreadsheet.

24Personal money, budgeting and compound interest
What they learn

The power of compound interest, live: what happens to ₪500 a month sitting in a current account versus an investment that earns a return — after 10, 20 and 30 years.

What they do

"The independent wealth map": building a personal monthly budget — income from work or pocket money, savings and spending — and a forward-looking personal savings plan.

Part 4The finale — the demo day project

25Pitch secrets for the sharks — the investor pitch and the brief
What they learn

How to face investors and raise money: the structure of a winning three-minute pitch — hook and problem, solution and product, market size and competitors, business model and profitability, the ask and the valuation. Each team gets the "Shark Tank challenge brief" for the finale.

What they do

"Defining the venture for investment": teams gather everything from the year in the project document — the need, the solution, the audience and the monetisation — and set the project title and fundraising goals.

26The financial report for the sharks — forecast and valuation
What they learn

How to show an investor financial data clearly: development and marketing costs, a sales forecast, gross and net profit, and how to work out what share of the company to offer the sharks for their investment (the ask and the valuation).

What they do

"Millionaire numbers": after focused work on the product, teams sum up their venture's financial data in one spreadsheet.

27The pitch deck sprint and dress rehearsal
What they learn

The rules of the three-minute pitch: body language, eye contact, smooth handovers between team members, and a strong message on clean slides without walls of text.

What they do

"Packing for investment and a dry run": in the first half teams build the investor deck with their live landing page and video; in the second they run quick simulations in pairs of teams for peer feedback.

28FutureGen Shark Tank — demo dayShowcase event

The annual finale in front of a panel of judges: parents, guests and mentors from the industry. Each team gives a three-minute pitch of a complete venture — the market need, the live site, the campaign film, the business model and the financial forecast — and answers questions and investment offers from the sharks.
